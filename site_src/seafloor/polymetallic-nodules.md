**Polymetallic (manganese) nodules** are concretions of manganese and iron oxides enriched in **nickel, copper, cobalt** and rare earths. They grow around a nucleus (such as a shark tooth or rock fragment) by a few millimetres **per million years**, so a nodule can be tens of millions of years old.

## Where

The richest field is the **Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ)** in the Pacific, an abyssal plain of more than 6 million km² at ~4,000–6,000 m. It is estimated to hold over 21 billion tonnes of nodules[^hw]. Other fields lie in the Indian Ocean (the Central Indian Ocean Basin, where India holds an ISA exploration contract) and the Peru Basin.

## Life on nodules

Nodules are hard islands on a soft plain. Many sponges, corals, anemones and xenophyophores **only** live attached to them. Removing nodules removes that habitat for geological timescales. After 44 years, a 1979 test-mining track still shows these attached animals barely returning[^jones]. See [Jones et al. 2025](/wiki/library/jones-2025.html).

## "Dark oxygen"

In 2024, benthic chamber experiments reported oxygen being **produced** on nodule fields. The authors proposed that nodule surfaces act like batteries, splitting seawater by electrolysis. The claim is contested, and a dedicated follow-up programme (DORI) began in 2026[^dori]. See [Sweetman et al. 2024](/wiki/library/sweetman-2024.html).

## The mining case

Nodules contain battery metals. Proponents argue nodule collection avoids some land-mining impacts. Opponents point to irreversible habitat loss, sediment plumes over wide areas, and unknown ecosystem functions. See [Deep-sea mining](/wiki/impacts/deep-sea-mining.html).
