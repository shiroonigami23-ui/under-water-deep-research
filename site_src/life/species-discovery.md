title: Species Discovery: How Many Ocean Species Are Still Unknown?
summary: The Ocean Census, eDNA and a new "discovered" status are speeding up the naming of ocean life, but most species remain undescribed.
level: Intermediate
---
About 240,000 marine species have been formally described. Estimates of the total range far higher, and some studies suggest **up to ~90% of ocean species remain undiscovered**[^gma].

## The Ocean Census

The Nippon Foundation–Nekton **Ocean Census** is the largest coordinated effort to speed up marine species discovery.

:::data Year 3 (1 Apr 2025 – 31 Mar 2026)
- **1,121** species new to science recorded, a **54% increase** over the usual annual rate[^sciam].
- 13 expeditions and 9 species-discovery workshops[^nekton].
- About two-thirds (728) came through Species Discovery Awards. Many came from specimens already sitting in museum collections[^sb].
:::

Highlights included new ghost sharks, a carnivorous *Chondrocladia* "death-ball" sponge at ~3,600 m, and a polychaete living inside a glass sponge at 791 m.

## The description bottleneck

Formal scientific description takes an average of **~13.5 years** from discovery to publication[^cp24]. To avoid species going extinct before they have a name, the Ocean Census launched **NOVA**, an open platform where "discovered" is recorded immediately as a formal status.

## New tools

- **eDNA:** genetic traces in water reveal species without capturing them.
- **4K and 8K ROV video** with AI-assisted annotation (for example MBARI's archives).
- **Integrative taxonomy:** combining genomes, micro-CT scans and morphology.

## Why it matters

Unnamed species cannot be protected or assessed. New species are being found in areas earmarked for **deep-sea mining**, where most fauna are undescribed. See [Deep-sea mining](/wiki/impacts/deep-sea-mining.html).
