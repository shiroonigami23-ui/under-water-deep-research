The ocean holds far more carbon than the atmosphere, land plants and soils combined[^grist]. It has absorbed roughly a **quarter** of the CO₂ humans emitted between the early 1960s and late 2010s[^me]. Part of that uptake is physical: CO₂ dissolves in cold water that then sinks. Part is **biological**, through the *biological carbon pump*.

## How the pump works

1. **Fixation.** Phytoplankton in the sunlit zone turn CO₂ into organic carbon by photosynthesis.
2. **Export.** A fraction sinks as **marine snow**: dead plankton, faecal pellets and mucus aggregates falling through the twilight zone.
3. **Attenuation.** Most of that snow is eaten or decomposed by microbes in the upper ~1,000 m, releasing CO₂ back into the water at depth.
4. **Sequestration.** Carbon that reaches the deep ocean stays out of contact with the atmosphere for **centuries**. Carbon that is buried in sediment can be locked away for **millennia or longer**.

:::facts The three "lanes" of the pump
- **Gravitational pump:** sinking particles (marine snow).
- **Migrant pump:** animals that feed at the surface at night and respire or excrete at depth during the day.
- **Mixing pump:** dissolved and suspended organic carbon carried down by seasonal mixing and subduction.
:::

## Why the twilight zone matters

How efficiently marine snow is consumed between 200 and 1,000 m largely decides how much carbon is sequestered. Small changes in that **attenuation** shift the ocean's carbon storage by large amounts. That is why the mesopelagic is a major research focus, and why proposals to fish twilight-zone animals at scale raise concern.

## The seafloor as a carbon store

Marine sediments, especially muddy coastal shelves and margins, hold enormous stocks of organic carbon. Disturbing them can remineralise that carbon back to CO₂:

- **Bottom trawling:** one modelling study estimated that 55–60% of the CO₂ released into seawater by trawling reaches the atmosphere within 7–9 years[^atwood]. The size of these emissions is actively debated (see [Bottom trawling](/wiki/impacts/bottom-trawling.html)).
- **Deep-sea mining** would disturb abyssal sediments, which are carbon-poor but extremely slow to recover.

## Feedbacks with climate change

- **Warming and stratification** reduce nutrient supply to the surface, which can weaken production in many regions.
- **Acidification** affects calcifying plankton, whose mineral shells help ballast sinking particles (see [Acidification](/wiki/climate/acidification.html)).
- **Deoxygenation** changes how organic matter is decomposed at depth.

## Open questions

- How large is the migrant pump globally? Estimates vary widely.
- Is ocean CO₂ uptake weakening as acidification intensifies? A 2025 study suggested it is[^me].
