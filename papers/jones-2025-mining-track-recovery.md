# Jones et al. (2025): Long-term impact and biological recovery in a deep-sea mining track

- **Citation:** Jones D.O.B., Arias M.B., et al. *Nature* (published 26 March 2025). doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08921-3
- **Access:** subscription (see the reading list for a full-text route)
- **Analysis basis:** press releases from NOC, NHM, Heriot-Watt, Liverpool and SAMS, plus coverage. Full text not read here.

## Question
What does a deep-sea nodule-mining disturbance look like after four decades, and which parts of the ecosystem recover?

## Method
- The SMARTEX project (NERC-funded) revisited a **1979 industrial test-mining track** in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (~5,000 m) in 2023 with RRS *James Cook* and the ROV *Isis*.
- Compared fauna, sediments and food (organic matter) inside the tracks with adjacent unmined seafloor and with nearby plume-affected areas.

## Key Results
- **Physical:** the tracks look much as they did when made. There is an ~8 m-wide strip cleared of nodules with two furrows from the collector.
- **Biological:** abundance and diversity are still substantially lower inside the tracks.
  - Some **small, mobile animals** on the sediment surface show the first signs of recovery.
  - **Xenophyophores** (giant single-celled organisms) have recolonised.
  - **Large attached fauna** such as sponges and corals, many of which need nodules to settle on, remain rare.
- **Food:** total food availability is similar, but **patchier inside the tracks**, which indicates disrupted food resources.
- Areas affected only by plumes showed more variable, often smaller effects.

## Strengths
- One of the longest real-world time series for a deep-sea mining impact.
- Multi-indicator: sediments, food webs and several faunal size classes.

## Limitations and Critiques
- A single small test track is far smaller than the hundreds of km² a commercial operation would disturb each year. Recovery from small-scale damage is likely *faster* than recovery at scale, because colonists can arrive from nearby.
- The authors note the results don't decide whether mining is acceptable; they inform protected-area design and monitoring.
- Nodules take millions of years to form, so nodule-dependent fauna cannot recover on human timescales whatever the sediment does.

## My Take
Both sides can quote this paper, and both readings are partly right. The clear result is that the *habitat-forming* layer (nodules plus the attached fauna on them) does not recover on decadal timescales. Any regulation that treats "recolonisation by mobile fauna" as recovery would be misreading it.

## Follow-ups
- Re-survey at 50+ years. Compare with the 2021–22 industry pilot-collector test sites for a modern-equipment baseline.
- Scale-dependence experiments: does recovery slow with disturbance width?
