# Portmann et al. (2026): Observational constraints project a ~50% AMOC weakening by the end of this century

- **Citation:** Portmann V., Swingedouw D., Khattab O., Chavent M. *Science Advances* 12(16) (April 2026). doi:10.1126/sciadv.adx4298
- **Access:** 🔓 Open access
- **Analysis basis:** abstract, CNN, Live Science, Science Focus and Science News coverage.

## Question
Climate models disagree widely on how much the AMOC will weaken by 2100. Can real-world observations narrow that range?

## Method
- Emergent-constraint style approach. It relates each model's simulated present-day ocean state (temperature and salinity across multiple variables) to its future AMOC change, then uses observations to pick out the most realistic behaviour.
- Corrects for model collinearity using **ridge-regularised linear regression**, which is rare in climate science.

## Key Results
- Projected weakening by 2100: **~43–59%** (central ~51%). That is about **60% stronger** than the unconstrained multi-model mean.
- Medium to very-high emission scenarios converge on ~50%. Only aggressive, sustained cuts give ~20%.
- Conclusion: the "pessimistic" high-weakening models are the realistic ones.

## Strengths
- Uses many observed variables at once instead of a single proxy.
- Substantially reduces uncertainty compared with the raw model spread.

## Limitations and Critiques
- Greenland meltwater is not included in the models, so this may underestimate weakening.
- It projects weakening, not collapse. The authors stress the distinction, and other experts note results vary widely between studies.
- Emergent constraints assume the model relationship holds in the future. If shared model biases exist, the constraint inherits them.

## My Take
The useful framing is "how much weakening is already baked in versus avoidable". The difference between ~50% and ~20% is the policy-relevant gap. I'd pair this paper with Holden et al. 2026 (committed-collapse probability) and the observed four-latitude decline to triangulate.
