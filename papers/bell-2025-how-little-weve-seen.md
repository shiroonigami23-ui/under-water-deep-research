# Bell et al. (2025): How little we've seen — a visual coverage estimate of the deep seafloor

- **Citation:** Bell K.L.C., Johannes K.N., Kennedy B.R.C., Poulton S.E. *Science Advances* 11(19) (7 May 2025). doi:10.1126/sciadv.adp8602
- **Access:** 🔓 Open access
- **Analysis basis:** abstract plus the Ocean Discovery League release and coverage.

## Question
What fraction of the deep seafloor (≥200 m) has ever been directly observed with cameras or human eyes, and where?

## Method
- Compiled a database of ~44,000 deep-sea dives with visual observations since 1958, from public records plus estimates for unreported private dives.
- Two complementary area estimates: a per-dive footprint by vehicle type, and a track-based method.

## Key Results
- Total area imaged ≈ **2,130–3,823 km²**, which is **≤0.001%** of the ~336 million km² deep seafloor (roughly a tenth of Belgium).
- **Geographic bias:** 65% of observations lie within 200 nmi of the **USA, Japan and New Zealand**. **97%** of dives come from five countries: USA, Japan, NZ, France and Germany.
- **Habitat bias:** canyons and ridges are over-sampled. Abyssal plains and seamounts are under-sampled. Only 442 of 9,472 known canyons have ever been seen, and Monterey Canyon alone accounts for ~48% of canyon observations.
- Even if the estimate were off by 10×, less than 0.01% would have any visual record.

## Strengths
- First quantitative global estimate; transparent about uncertainty.
- Exposes structural inequity in who explores the ocean.

## Limitations and Critiques
- Private and military dive records are unknown, which is why the authors give an order-of-magnitude buffer.
- "Seen" doesn't equal "characterised". A quick camera pass gives little ecological information.

## My Take
This is the most important context number in the hub. Every generalisation about deep-sea life rests on a sample smaller than a mid-size city, drawn mostly from three countries' waters. It argues for low-cost, distributed exploration tools and for humility in mining impact assessments.

## Follow-ups
- Re-run the estimate in 2030 to test whether new low-cost tools and Ocean Census expeditions bend the curve.
