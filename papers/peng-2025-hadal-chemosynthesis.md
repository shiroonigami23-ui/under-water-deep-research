# Peng, Du et al. (2025): Flourishing chemosynthetic life at the greatest depths of hadal trenches

- **Citation:** Peng X., Du M., Gebruk A., Mordukhovich V., et al. *Nature* (published 30 July 2025). doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09317-z
- **Access:** 🔓 Open access
- **Analysis basis:** abstract, *Nature* press highlight, IDSSE/CAS release and independent coverage. Full text not read here.

## Question
Do hadal trenches (6,000–11,000 m) support chemosynthesis-based animal communities, and how deep and widespread are they?

## Method
- Crewed full-ocean-depth submersible ***Fendouzhe*** (IDSSE, CAS).
- Expedition 8 July – 17 August 2024 along the Kuril–Kamchatka Trench, the Kamchatka–Aleutian transition and the western Aleutian Trench.
- Visual surveys, sampling, and isotopic and geochemical analysis of seep fluids and sediments.

## Key Results
- Dense communities dominated by **siboglinid tubeworms and bivalves**, fed by hydrogen sulfide and methane seeping from faults where the Pacific Plate bends into the trench.
- Found over a ~2,500 km region at **5,800–9,533 m**. The deepest site ("The Deepest") is in the Kuril–Kamchatka Trench, at ~98 MPa.
- Up to ~5,800 tubeworms per m² reported at some sites.
- Sediment methane was unusually high, consistent with **microbes producing methane in place** from buried organic carbon. That points to a deep, local carbon recycling loop.
- The authors suggest similar communities may exist in other trenches with similar geology.

## Strengths
- Direct, crewed observation at record depth, rather than inference from trawls.
- Large spatial extent (thousands of km) rather than a single spot.
- Combines biology with fluid geochemistry, which explains *why* life is there.

## Limitations and Critiques
- It describes one northwest Pacific region. As one independent commentary stressed, it is a regional description, not a global inventory, and it does not quantify the global carbon-cycle role.
- "Up to 5,800 per m²" is a local peak, not a trench-wide average.
- Species-level taxonomy of many specimens is likely still in progress.

## My Take
This moves hadal chemosynthesis from "a few clam patches" to "possibly a trench-scale ecosystem type". The most important new idea is the *in-situ microbial methane* supply. If confirmed elsewhere, trenches are not only sinks for carbon but active bioreactors, and that matters for both carbon budgets and astrobiology analogues.

## Follow-ups
- Repeat surveys in other subduction trenches (Japan, Kermadec, Atacama).
- Quantify methane flux and the fraction derived from microbes.
- Compare tubeworm symbiont genomes to vent and seep relatives for pressure adaptation (link to the `mutation/` track).
