AMOC Weakening

The Atlantic overturning that ventilates the deep North Atlantic is weakening, with new projections of 43–59% decline by 2100 and a live debate about committed collapse.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 1 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Advanced

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) carries warm surface water north. There it cools, sinks and flows back south at depth, delivering oxygen and carbon to the deep Atlantic (see Deep circulation).

What the 2026 evidence says#

  • Observed: weakening at four latitudes over two decades (University of Miami study, April 2026).
  • Projected (Portmann et al. 2026, Science Advances): combining models with temperature and salinity observations gives 43–59% weakening by 2100 (central ≈51%). That is about 60% stronger than the multi-model average. Aggressive emission cuts reduce it to about 20%. Greenland meltwater is not included, so this may be an underestimate12.
  • Committed collapse? A July 2026 ensemble study argues there is a significant probability that collapse is already locked in3.
Where scientists disagree

The IPCC AR6 assessed collapse before 2100 as “very unlikely” (medium confidence). Newer observation-constrained studies argue the risk is higher. Disagreements centre on model biases in salinity, how to read short observational records, and early-warning statistics from proxy data.

Consequences of a major slowdown#

  • Colder winters in northwest Europe, despite global warming.
  • Faster sea-level rise along the US East Coast.
  • Shifted tropical rain belts, affecting monsoons in West Africa and South Asia.
  • Less oxygen and carbon reaching the deep North Atlantic, affecting cold-water coral reefs and deep fauna.
  • Reduced ocean uptake of CO₂.

Full analysis of the paper: Portmann et al. 2026.
