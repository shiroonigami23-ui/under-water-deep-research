Ocean Deoxygenation

The ocean has lost more than 2% of its oxygen since 1960, the volume of anoxic water has quadrupled, and oxygen minimum zones are growing.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 5 sources🎓 Intermediate

Oxygen loss is the least-known of the ocean’s three climate stressors (with warming and acidification), but it may be just as dangerous.

Key measurements
  • Global ocean oxygen fell by more than 2% (4.8 ± 2.1 petamoles) between 1960 and 2010, from a total of ~227 petamoles (Schmidtko et al. 2017, Nature)1.
  • The volume of oxygen-free water in the open ocean has quadrupled2.
  • Oxygen minimum zones at 200 m expanded by ~4.5 million km², roughly 60% of Australia’s area3.
  • Coastal areas at risk of dead zones have grown more than tenfold since 19502.
  • Losses could reach up to 7% by 21004.

Why oxygen is falling#

  1. Solubility: warm water holds less gas. This explains about 15% of the loss1.
  2. Stratification: a warmer surface layer mixes less with deeper water, so less oxygen is carried down. Weaker overturning (see AMOC weakening) adds to this.
  3. Nutrient pollution (coastal): fertiliser and sewage fuel algal blooms whose decay consumes oxygen.

Oxygen minimum zones (OMZs)#

OMZs are natural mid-water layers (roughly 100–1,000 m) beneath productive surface waters, especially in the eastern tropical Pacific, Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. As they expand:

  • Tuna, billfish and sharks are squeezed into shallower water, where they are easier to catch.
  • Microbes in OMZs produce nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas, and remove fixed nitrogen.
  • Specialists such as the vampire squid thrive in low oxygen, but most animals cannot.

The deep connection#

Deep water gets oxygen only where it sinks from the surface at high latitudes. Slower overturning means deep habitats such as cold-water coral reefs could lose oxygen, though the size of that effect this century is still debated.

Can it reverse?#

Some models suggest OMZs may start shrinking from 2150–2300, but only after centuries5. Reducing nutrient runoff can shrink coastal dead zones quickly. Open-ocean deoxygenation needs emission cuts.
