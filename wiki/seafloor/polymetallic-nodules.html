Polymetallic Nodules

Potato-sized mineral lumps that grow a few millimetres per million years, now at the centre of the deep-sea mining fight.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 1 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Intermediate

Polymetallic (manganese) nodules are concretions of manganese and iron oxides enriched in nickel, copper, cobalt and rare earths. They grow around a nucleus (such as a shark tooth or rock fragment) by a few millimetres per million years, so a nodule can be tens of millions of years old.

Where#

The richest field is the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) in the Pacific, an abyssal plain of more than 6 million km² at ~4,000–6,000 m. It is estimated to hold over 21 billion tonnes of nodules1. Other fields lie in the Indian Ocean (the Central Indian Ocean Basin, where India holds an ISA exploration contract) and the Peru Basin.

Life on nodules#

Nodules are hard islands on a soft plain. Many sponges, corals, anemones and xenophyophores only live attached to them. Removing nodules removes that habitat for geological timescales. After 44 years, a 1979 test-mining track still shows these attached animals barely returning2. See Jones et al. 2025.

“Dark oxygen”#

In 2024, benthic chamber experiments reported oxygen being produced on nodule fields. The authors proposed that nodule surfaces act like batteries, splitting seawater by electrolysis. The claim is contested, and a dedicated follow-up programme (DORI) began in 20263. See Sweetman et al. 2024.

The mining case#

Nodules contain battery metals. Proponents argue nodule collection avoids some land-mining impacts. Opponents point to irreversible habitat loss, sediment plumes over wide areas, and unknown ecosystem functions. See Deep-sea mining.
