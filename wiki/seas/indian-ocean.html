🧭 At a glance

Indian Ocean covers about 70,560,000 km², averages 3,741 m deep and reaches 7,290 m at Java (Sunda) Trench. This encyclopedia links 406 species to it, including Abyssal grenadier, Abyssobrotula galatheae, Albacore, Banded pipefish, Basking shark, Bigfin squid, Black swallower, Blue shark. This encyclopedia links 4 rivers to it, including Brahmaputra, Ganges (Ganga), Hooghly, Mahanadi. Wrecks and discoveries here: Batavia, SS Tilawa, Malaysia Airlines MH370 search.

Overview#

The Indian Ocean (~70.5 million km²) is the third-largest and the warmest ocean. Unlike the Pacific and Atlantic, it is closed to the north by Asia, so it has no northern polar water. Instead its circulation is ruled by the monsoon: winds and major currents reverse direction twice a year. It is named after India, whose rainfall, agriculture and economy depend directly on its behaviour.

Geography#

Bounded by Africa to the west, Asia to the north, Australia and Indonesia to the east and the Southern Ocean to the south. Marginal seas include the Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal, Andaman Sea, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Laccadive Sea and Mozambique Channel. Island groups include Madagascar, Sri Lanka, the Maldives, Lakshadweep, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Seychelles, Mauritius and Réunion. Major rivers include the Ganges–Brahmaputra, Indus, Zambezi and Irrawaddy.

Seafloor and geology#

The Java (Sunda) Trench (~7,290 m) is its deepest point and the source of the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman earthquake and tsunami. Three ridges (Central, Southwest and Southeast Indian ridges) meet at the Rodrigues Triple Junction, where the Kairei vent field hosts the scaly-foot snail. The Bengal Fan, built by sediment from the Himalaya via the Ganges–Brahmaputra, is the largest submarine fan on Earth. The Central Indian Ocean Basin holds nodule fields explored by India under an ISA contract.

Water and currents#

During the southwest monsoon (June–September) the Somali Current flows north at up to ~3.5 m/s and drives strong upwelling off Somalia, Oman and western India; in the northeast monsoon it reverses. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), a see-saw of temperatures between west and east, shapes rainfall in India, East Africa and Australia. The Agulhas Current, one of the strongest western boundary currents, leaks warm salty water into the Atlantic. The Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal hold intense oxygen minimum zones.

Climate, weather and cyclones#

The Bay of Bengal produces some of the deadliest tropical cyclones in history, because a shallow, funnel-shaped bay amplifies storm surges onto the low Ganges delta. Arabian Sea cyclones have become more frequent and intense as it warms. Monsoon rain from the ocean supplies most of India’s annual rainfall. The Indian Ocean is warming faster than any other ocean, intensifying marine heatwaves.

Flora and fauna#

Coelacanths off South Africa and Comoros, whale sharks of the Maldives and Ningaloo, dugongs, olive ridley turtles mass-nesting in Odisha, the Sundarbans mangroves with their tigers and Ganges river dolphins, manta rays of the Maldives, hilsa migrating up the Bengal rivers, and vent fauna like the scaly-foot snail.

Ecosystems#

Coral atolls (Maldives, Lakshadweep, Chagos), the Sundarbans (the world’s largest mangrove forest), seagrass meadows grazed by dugongs, upwelling systems, deep oxygen minimum zones and hydrothermal vents.

History and exploration#

Monsoon winds powered trade between Africa, Arabia, India and China for over 2,000 years. Vasco da Gama reached India by sea in 1498. The 2004 tsunami killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries and led to the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System.

People and economy#

Fisheries feed hundreds of millions of people. It carries major oil routes through the Strait of Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb, and about 80% of global seaborne oil trade passes through it. India’s Deep Ocean Mission and Samudrayaan target its depths.

Threats and protection#

Fastest warming of any ocean, repeated coral bleaching (the Maldives and Lakshadweep), expanding oxygen minimum zones in the Arabian Sea, sea-level rise threatening the Maldives, plastics carried by rivers, and overfishing.
