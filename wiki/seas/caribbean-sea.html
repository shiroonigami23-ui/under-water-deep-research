🏝 Caribbean Sea

Atlantic Ocean

A tropical sea of coral reefs and the deepest point of the Caribbean, the Cayman Trough.

🧭 At a glance

Caribbean Sea covers about 2,754,000 km², averages 2,200 m deep and reaches 7,686 m at Cayman Trough. This encyclopedia links 109 species to it, including Albacore, American lobster, Bluehead wrasse, Dwarf lanternshark, French angelfish, Great barracuda, Great hammerhead, Greater argonaut. Wrecks and discoveries here: San José galleon.

Overview#

A tropical sea of about 2.75 million km² bounded by the Antilles island arcs, Central America and northern South America. It is famous for coral reefs, clear blue water and hurricanes, and for being the first region of the Americas reached by Columbus in 1492.

Geography#

It lies on the Caribbean plate, framed by the Greater Antilles (Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, Puerto Rico) to the north and the Lesser Antilles volcanic arc to the east. It connects to the Gulf of Mexico through the Yucatán Channel and to the Pacific via the Panama Canal.

Seafloor and geology#

Its deepest point is the Cayman Trough (~7,686 m), where an ultra-slow spreading centre hosts the world’s deepest known hydrothermal vents (Beebe vent field, ~5,000 m). The Puerto Rico Trench just to the north is the deepest point of the Atlantic.

Water and currents#

Warm (26–29 °C), clear and low in nutrients. The Caribbean Current carries water westward into the Gulf of Mexico, where it becomes the Loop Current and feeds the Gulf Stream.

Climate, weather and cyclones#

The core of the Atlantic hurricane belt: warm water (>26.5°C) fuels storms from June to November. Sargassum influxes since 2011 smother beaches. Repeated heatwaves have bleached most reefs.

Flora and fauna#

Home to the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef (the second-largest barrier reef), staghorn and elkhorn corals, parrotfish, groupers, manatees, hawksbill and green turtles, and the vast seasonal mats of drifting Sargassum seaweed.

Ecosystems#

Fringing and barrier reefs, mangrove forests, seagrass beds, blue holes (such as Belize’s Great Blue Hole) and deep-sea vents. Many reefs have lost over half their coral cover since the 1970s.

History and exploration#

Indigenous Taíno and Kalinago peoples lived on the islands before European conquest. The sea became the centre of the plantation economy and the transatlantic slave trade, and the age of pirates; wrecks of Spanish treasure galleons still lie on its floor.

People and economy#

Tourism is the main industry for many island nations, alongside fishing and shipping through the Panama Canal.

Threats and protection#

Coral bleaching (mass events in 2005, 2010, 2023), stony coral tissue loss disease, invasive lionfish, overfishing, Sargassum influxes and stronger hurricanes.
