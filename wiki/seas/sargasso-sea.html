🌿 Sargasso Sea

Atlantic Ocean

The only sea without coastlines, defined by currents and floating Sargassum seaweed.

TypeSea
Part ofAtlantic Ocean
Area (km²)4,100,000
Deepest point—
Salinity~36.5 PSU

🧭 At a glance

Sargasso Sea covers about 4,100,000 km². This encyclopedia links 60 species to it, including American lobster, Acorn worm, American eel, Armored searobin, Arrow crab, Atlantic blue crab, Atlantic croaker, Atlantic sea nettle.

Overview#

The only sea without a coastline, defined by ocean currents rather than land. It lies in the North Atlantic subtropical gyre and is named after Sargassum, the golden-brown seaweed that floats in vast mats on its surface.

Geography#

Bounded by the Gulf Stream to the west, the North Atlantic Current to the north, the Canary Current to the east and the North Atlantic Equatorial Current to the south. Bermuda lies on its western edge.

Seafloor and geology#

Overlies deep abyssal plains (Nares and Hatteras) and the Bermuda Rise; about 4,500–6,500 m deep.

Water and currents#

Exceptionally clear, deep-blue, warm and nutrient-poor, with very little plankton. Being at the centre of a gyre, it gathers floating debris, part of the North Atlantic garbage patch.

Climate, weather and cyclones#

Bounded only by currents of the North Atlantic Gyre, so it has calm, clear, nutrient-poor warm water. The Bermuda Atlantic Time-series Study (since 1988) records ocean warming and acidification here.

Flora and fauna#

Sargassum mats shelter a unique community: the Sargassum fish, Sargassum shrimp and crabs, juvenile sea turtles and baby fish. European and American eels migrate thousands of kilometres to spawn here, a mystery first solved by Johannes Schmidt in the 1920s.

Ecosystems#

Floating sargassum mats host endemic sargassum fish, crabs and nudibranchs, and nurseries for sea turtles. It is the spawning ground of European and American eels.

History and exploration#

Named by Portuguese sailors; Columbus feared his ships would be trapped in its weed. The 2014 Hamilton Declaration created a commission for its conservation.

People and economy#

Little direct use; it is a key area for long-term ocean monitoring at the Bermuda Atlantic Time-series Study (BATS), running since 1988.

Threats and protection#

Plastic accumulation, shipping, and loss of eels whose numbers have collapsed by over 90% in some rivers. The Hamilton Declaration (2014) set up an international commission to protect it.
