Mapping the Seafloor

Why we have better maps of Mars than of our own seafloor, how multibeam sonar works, and whether Seabed 2030 can finish the job.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 1 min read🔗 2 sources🎓 Intermediate

Methods#

  • Satellite altimetry: sea-surface height bulges over seamounts and dips over trenches. This gives a global map, but only at ~kilometre resolution.
  • Multibeam echo sounding: ships emit a fan of sound beams and time the echoes, mapping a swath several times the water depth wide at ~100 m resolution or better in the deep sea.
  • AUV mapping: flying close to the seabed for metre-scale resolution.
  • Crowd-sourced bathymetry: fishing boats, yachts and commercial ships contribute depth data.

Seabed 2030 progress#

Numbers
  • 6% mapped to modern standards at launch in 20171.
  • 27.3% by June 20251.
  • 28.7% by April 2026, with almost five million km² added in a year, about 104 million km² in total2.

Will it finish by 2030?#

At ~1.5 percentage points per year, no. Reaching 100% would need roughly an order-of-magnitude acceleration. Uncrewed surface vessels, data-sharing from industry and militaries, and crowd-sourcing are the main levers. Even complete mapping at ~100 m resolution would not show what lives there; imaging coverage is far smaller (see How little we have seen).

Why maps matter#

Tsunami modelling, cable routing, navigation, fisheries habitat models, climate models (bottom topography steers currents) and deciding where to protect.
